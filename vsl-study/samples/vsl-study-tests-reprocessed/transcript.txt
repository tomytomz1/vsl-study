A few years ago, when Hurricane Marcus devastated the Gulf Coast, leaving 1.8 million families without power for weeks, emergency crews were using drones to survey the destruction, searching for signs of life in neighborhoods that looked like war zones. That's when rescue coordinator Sarah Chen noticed something impossible. One small house was glowing with warm light, while everything else for miles around sat in complete darkness. When the emergency team finally reached that address, what they discovered defied all logic. 82-year-old retired mechanic Tom Rodriguez was peacefully reading his evening paper. Lights on throughout the house, television broadcasting the weather report, even his old chest freezer keeping his ice cream frozen solid. Meanwhile, his neighbors were huddled around candles, wondering if they'd have power again before Christmas. But here's the part that makes absolutely no sense. There was no backup generator humming in his yard, no expensive solar array on his roof, and the main power cables to his street had been completely severed by a massive pine tree. When Sarah asked Tom how his lights were still working, the old man just smiled and said he'd figured out a little trick years ago. That little trick was about to reveal the most closely guarded secret in the energy industry. Four days later, when utility repair crews finally arrived to restore power to the neighborhood, their diagnostic equipment started acting erratic. The power meters were running in reverse. Tom wasn't just consuming electricity, he was producing it. So much surplus energy that his home was actually heating power back into the damaged grid. The lead technician stared at his readings in complete shock, informing Tom that the utility company now owed him $387 for the excess electricity he'd generated during the blackout. But that $387 payment was just the beginning. Over the following eight months, the power company sent Tom checks totaling $2,156. And Tom isn't some brilliant engineer with it.
